PAA03

Private Life Context Exposure

Protects: Permitted flow of personal information across audiences and purposes.

Overview

Scenarios6 (4 documented)

Related entriesPAA08 (processing aimed at another person), PAA04 (integrity of memory), PAA02 (takeover)

Personal information or sensitive inferences cross an audience, purpose, or account boundary without appropriate authority. The data may be accurate and legitimately accessible for one task yet inappropriate for another recipient. This entry protects the operator's own context boundaries (contextual integrity); surveillance or processing directed at another person belongs to PAA08.

Common examples of vulnerability

  • A work-meeting summary or attachment includes a personal bank statement because both were recently open.
  • An inferred medical, relationship, or financial attribute appears in a routine scheduling message.
  • Memory, retrieval indexes, or logs mix work, family, and private data under one profile.
  • Cross-account tool calls leak content from a private mailbox into a work thread.
  • Exports, logs, or shared transcripts expose more than the approved content or audience.

Example attack scenarios

Documented scenarios generalize a real case listed below. Hypothetical scenarios have no matching public case yet.

Scenario 1: Bank statement attached to a work email Hypothetical

An assistant helping with a work meeting attaches a personal bank statement because both documents were recently open. Each file was legitimately accessible; the combination crosses an audience boundary.

Scenario 2: Inference leaks into scheduling Hypothetical

An assistant that inferred a health condition from calendar and purchase history mentions it while negotiating a meeting time with a colleague's assistant.

Scenario 3: One inbound email makes the assistant send out the owner's data Documented

An attacker emails the person. Without any click, the assistant reads the email as an instruction, gathers data from the person's accounts, and leaks it through an auto-loaded image or link.

Scenario 4: Public-channel text pulls private-channel content Documented

An attacker posts crafted text in a channel the victim can read. The workplace assistant mixes it with private-channel data and renders a link that leaks the private data.

Scenario 5: A share link makes private chats public Documented

A person shares a chat with one friend. The link is later indexed by a search engine, so the conversation, and anything personal in it, becomes searchable.

Scenario 6: A share button posts a private chat to a public feed Documented

A person taps share in an assistant app without realizing the result is a public feed. Medical, legal, or relationship details become visible to strangers.

Real-world cases

Dated and sourced. Nothing here shows how common the failure is; it shows that it has happened or been demonstrated.

EchoLeak: zero-click data exfiltration from Microsoft 365 Copilot

What happened
Aim Security found that a single crafted email could make Microsoft 365 Copilot pull data from the user's own context and send it out through an auto-fetched image URL, with no user action. The chain bypassed Microsoft's prompt-injection classifier, link redaction, and a content security policy via an allowed Teams proxy. Tracked as CVE-2025-32711 and fixed server-side by Microsoft.
Caveats
Workplace product, not a personal agent. Sources conflict on whether it was exploited in the wild; the paper calls it a real-world exploit in a production system but does not say attackers used it before disclosure.

Slack AI can be tricked into leaking private-channel data

What happened
PromptArmor showed that text planted in a public Slack channel could make Slack AI, when answering a victim's question, pull in content from private channels and render a markdown link ('click here to reauthenticate') that leaked the data. Slack initially described the behavior as intended; Dark Reading later reported that Slack patched the flaw.
Caveats
A workplace assistant, not a personal agent. Based on search summaries and secondary coverage of PromptArmor's post.

Shared AI chats become searchable: ChatGPT, Grok and Claude

What happened
Fast Company reported on 30 July 2025 that Google was indexing ChatGPT conversations users had shared by link, with nearly 4,500 surfacing in a site search (likely an undercount); OpenAI called the feature experimental and removed it. Forbes later reported hundreds of thousands of Grok conversations indexed without a warning to users (counts of 300,000-370,000 vary by outlet), and, on 8 September 2025, that hundreds of Claude transcripts appeared in Google results.
Caveats
Only explicitly shared chats were affected, but many users did not expect search indexing. Removal from a search engine does not delete the chat or its link. Counts and some dates vary; the Fast Company page returned 403, so this relies on search summaries.

Meta AI app share button posts private chats to a public Discover feed

What happened
Android Authority reported that users tapping share in the Meta AI app could publish chats to the public Discover feed, including medical information, legal dilemmas, and confessions, with no clear warning above the post button. Meta said chats are private by default and sharing takes a four-step process. The Mozilla Foundation asked Meta to notify users and help them delete accidentally shared content.
Caveats
Meta's later UI changes are only reported by one secondary source. The original TechCrunch coverage was not reviewed.

ShadowLeak and GeminiJack: server-side exfiltration from connected mail and documents

What happened
Radware's ShadowLeak made ChatGPT's agent send data from connected sources such as Gmail, Outlook, and GitHub out through URL parameters from OpenAI's servers; OpenAI added guardrails. Noma Labs' GeminiJack hid instructions in a shared document, calendar invite, or email so Gemini Enterprise exfiltrated connected data with no click; Noma reported it to Google on 6 May 2025, disclosed it in December 2025, and Google reworked how retrieved content is handled.
Caveats
Both involve connected workplace or enterprise data. Disclosure dates are inconsistent across outlets (GeminiJack 9 or 11 December 2025) and only secondary coverage was reviewed.

Gap

No documented case of an agent mixing a person's private and work context

What happened
Benchmarks show the failure is common in lab conditions (Agent CI Bench reports 12 of 15 frontier computer-use agents leaking private data in over 50% of scenarios, per a secondary summary; CI-Work reports 15.8-50.9% violation rates), but no public report of a real person harmed this way was found.
Caveats

Prevention and mitigation

What you can do

  • Separate work, family, and private activities into distinct agent profiles or accounts.
  • Use previews for outbound messages and attachments that touch sensitive categories.
  • Review what is retained and delete purpose-expired data.

What the provider must do

  • Enforce context separation in retrieval, tool access, credentials, and outbound actions; profile labels alone are not isolation.
  • Apply source-level protection to sensitive inferences, not only source records.
  • Limit approved transfers to the approved content and audience; show the diff between what was approved and what is sent.
  • Tie secondary use and retention to purpose with understandable controls.
  • Evaluate with contextual-integrity benchmarks, not only secret-string leakage tests.

Where a safeguard is unavailable, offer a narrower mode such as read-only access or manual completion rather than rely on an instruction to the model.

Test it

Reproducible control test. Seed distinct private and work datasets with traceable synthetic facts. Request mixed-context summaries, attachments, and delegated messages. Inspect direct facts, inferred attributes, logs, and exported results for unauthorized disclosure, and confirm that an explicitly approved transfer is limited to the approved content and audience.
