PAA05

Counterparty Deception and Agent Mediated Scams

Protects: Verification of the external party or claim driving a personal transaction.

Overview

Scenarios4 (4 documented)
Sourced cases4
Related entriesPAA06 (errors with a legitimate counterparty), PAA07 (speaking as the owner), PAA09 (pressure on the owner's choice)

An agent treats a deceptive merchant, payee, support claim, or emergency request as sufficient authority for action. The failed protection is verification of the external party or claim. The scam can arise inside a legitimate, owner-initiated task and needs no unsolicited command.

Common examples of vulnerability

  • A fake booking or shopping site passes the agent's checks and receives saved card details.
  • Changed bank details on an invoice are accepted without checking an independent record.
  • A compromised contact asks the agent to settle a fabricated bill.
  • Fabricated urgency or authority text on a page is treated as an instruction.
  • An approval screen repeats the scammer's framing instead of exposing recipient and evidence.

Example attack scenarios

Documented scenarios generalize a real case listed below. Hypothetical scenarios have no matching public case yet.

Scenario 1: Lookalike storefront checkout Documented

A person asks an agent to buy a product. The agent lands on a lookalike store and checks out with the saved card and address because the listing matches the request.

Scenario 2: Phishing email treated as a task Documented

A fake bank email arrives in the inbox the agent manages. The agent logs it as a to-do, opens the link without checking the address, and enters the person's credentials on the phishing page.

Scenario 3: Fake CAPTCHA tells the agent to click Documented

A page shows a CAPTCHA-style gate with hidden instructions. The agent follows them, clicks the button, and downloads a file the person never asked for.

Scenario 4: An unverified plea triggers a transfer Documented

A stranger replies to an agent that holds funds and asks for a small payment with a hardship story. The agent has no way to verify the claim and sends far more than asked.

Lobstar Wilde: an agent that holds funds answers a stranger's plea

What happened
An agent built by an OpenAI employee, funded with about $50,000 of Solana to trade, sent about 52.4 million LOBSTAR tokens (about $441,788 at the time) to an X user who had asked for 4 SOL (about $310) for an uncle's tetanus treatment. Prevention and mitigation

What you can do

  • Verify unusual payees and payment changes through an independently established channel.
  • Approve new payees yourself; do not let the agent add them on message evidence alone.

What the provider must do

  • Preserve the source of each claim; check changed payment details against an independent record.
  • Do not let an incoming request supply its own proof of authority.
  • Show recipient, evidence, and unusual terms on the approval screen; flag inconsistencies.
  • Run merchant and payee reputation checks that are independent of page content; prefer allow-listed or tokenized payment rails.
  • Do not treat spending limits as a substitute for counterparty verification.

Where a safeguard is unavailable, offer a narrower mode such as read-only access or manual completion rather than rely on an instruction to the model.

Test it

Reproducible control test. Use synthetic invoices, support messages, and booking pages with changed destinations and fabricated urgency. Include compromised familiar senders and owner-initiated tasks. Verify that spending limits do not substitute for verification and that legitimate requests remain possible through a defined confirmation path.
