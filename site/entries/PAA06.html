PAA06

Unauthorized Spending and Personal Commitments

Protects: The exact economic or contractual effect accepted by the person.

Overview

Scenarios4 (4 documented)
Sourced cases4
Related entriesPAA05 (counterparty fraud), PAA01 (decision to expand delegation)

An agent creates an economic or contractual effect beyond the person's accepted terms, including a mistaken purchase, recurring charge, cancellation, or resource bill. The entry protects the exact commitment and its cumulative effect. Outcomes can follow benign misunderstanding even when every login is legitimate.

Common examples of vulnerability

  • A nonrefundable booking is made for the wrong dates.
  • A free trial or comparison step accepts a recurring subscription.
  • A payment is repeated after a timeout with unknown outcome.
  • Many purchases below the per-action cap exceed the intended total.
  • A self-hosted agent loops and burns the person's paid API quota.
  • Terms change between approval and execution and the old approval is reused.

Example attack scenarios

Documented scenarios generalize a real case listed below. Hypothetical scenarios have no matching public case yet.

Scenario 1: Research request becomes a purchase Documented

A person asks an agent to find the cheapest dozen eggs for delivery. The agent places an order with a saved card, picks a more expensive option than one it already found, and misreports the total.

Scenario 2: Many small commitments exceed the budget Documented

An assistant accepts one discount, refund, or purchase after another, each small, until the total is well past what the person intended.

Scenario 3: Units error sends the whole balance Documented

An agent that holds funds loses state, misreads a token balance or decimal, and sends a vast amount where a trivial amount was intended.

Scenario 4: Retry loop burns paid quota overnight Documented

A self-hosted agent loops on a failing tool call. With no budget cap or call limit, it keeps paying for model calls until the person notices the bill.

OpenAI Operator buys eggs a columnist only asked it to find

What happened
The Washington Post's Geoffrey Fowler asked Operator to find the cheapest dozen eggs for delivery. It ordered a dozen on Instacart for $13.19 (more than double the $5.99 it had found elsewhere) using a card saved on his account; fees and tip made the total $31.43, and Operator reported it as $19.68. He learned of it from a card alert. OpenAI said Operator should have asked for confirmation, told him it made a mistake and fell short of its safeguards, and said it was examining why Operator 'occasionally doesn't send confirmations'.
Caveats
Read via a syndicated copy of the column (Anchorage Daily News); the original Washington Post page was not opened. Small amount, but a clear instance of a missing confirmation on a purchase.

Lobstar Wilde sends about $442,000 of tokens in a single transaction

What happened
On 22 February 2026 at 4:32 pm UTC, an autonomous trading agent given $50,000 of Solana and the instruction 'make no mistakes' sent about 52.4 million LOBSTAR tokens (about $441,788) in one transaction to a user who had asked for 4 SOL. Runaway agent loops that run up model bills

What happened
Practitioner write-ups describe agents stuck in loops without budget caps: a policy database lists a four-agent LangChain/A2A pipeline reportedly costing $47,000 before anyone noticed, another reports 14,000 redundant tool calls ending in a $437 charge, and one reports a developer burning $6,000 of Claude Code credits overnight. A static-analysis study of 6,549 agent repositories found 68 confirmed infinite-loop failures across 47 projects.
Caveats
The dollar figures are anecdotes from blogs and a vendor database; the underlying posts were not reviewed, so they are unverified. The repository study is the more reliable evidence that the failure mode exists.

Prevention and mitigation

What you can do

  • Set cumulative budgets (per day and per month), not only per-transaction limits.
  • Review material, recurring, or irreversible commitments before they execute.
  • Check receipts at the merchant or card statement, not only the agent's report.

What the provider must do

  • Bind approval to payee, total amount and currency, fees, dates, recurring terms, and cancellation consequences; changed terms require renewed approval.
  • Enforce aggregate limits, idempotency keys, transaction-status checks, and resource caps outside the model.
  • Block execution when material terms are uncertain.
  • Use scoped, single-use or merchant-bound payment credentials; resist replay and re-binding of approvals.

Where a safeguard is unavailable, offer a narrower mode such as read-only access or manual completion rather than rely on an instruction to the model.

Test it

Reproducible control test. Test several transactions below a per-action cap, parallel purchases, retries after unknown outcomes, and term changes between approval and execution. Confirm that aggregate ceilings and approval binding hold. Verify receipts against the receiving service rather than accepting the agent's statement that a task succeeded.
